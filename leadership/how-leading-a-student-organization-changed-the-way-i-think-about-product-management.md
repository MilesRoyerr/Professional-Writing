# How Leading a Student Organization Changed the Way I Think About Product Management

**Author:** Miles Royer  
**Originally published:** October 26, 2026  
**Category:** Leadership & Product Management  
**Original publication:** [Read the article on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/how-leading-student-organization-changed-way-i-think-product-royer-gpbnc)

---

When I transferred to the University of Wisconsin-Whitewater, I planned to join the ACM (Association for Computing Machinery) as a member.

I didn't plan to become its president.

At the time, ACM had around five active members and hadn't held a meeting in roughly six months. Many students didn't know the organization existed, and it was clear that getting things moving again would take some work.

I saw an opportunity to help.

I started promoting ACM, helped recruit officers, organized meetings and workshops, and worked with other students to rebuild the organization. Over time, we grew from around five active members to approximately 30.

I'm proud of that growth, but looking back, the number isn't what stands out most to me. It's the people I met, the community we built, and the lessons I learned along the way.

Those experiences also changed how I think about product management, a field I've become increasingly interested in through my internships and work with technical teams.

Here are three lessons from leading ACM that continue to shape how I think about building things for people.

## 1. Start by understanding what people actually want

When we started bringing ACM back to life, we could have simply planned a series of technical presentations and hoped people showed up.

Instead, we needed to understand what students wanted from the organization.

We held a meeting to gather ideas from members. Students suggested everything from web design and GitHub workshops to résumé help, career development, and an ACM website. Those conversations helped shape the activities we organized, including a LinkedIn workshop I hosted.

That experience taught me something simple: you can have plenty of ideas and still miss the mark if you don't understand the people you're trying to help.

It's a lesson that translates directly to product management.

Product managers help determine what a product should accomplish, which problems it should solve, and what a team should prioritize. That work starts with understanding users rather than assuming you already know what they need.

A feature might sound great in a planning meeting, but does it solve a real problem? Is it something users actually want? Are we addressing the right issue in the first place?

Those are questions worth asking before investing time in a solution.

With ACM, listening to members helped us plan activities people were interested in attending. In product management, that same habit of listening can help teams focus on building something genuinely useful.

## 2. Good ideas need people who can turn them into reality

Having ideas was only the beginning. We still needed people to organize workshops, promote events, coordinate schedules, and make everything happen.

That meant building a leadership team and learning how to work with people who brought different strengths to the organization. Our officers each contributed in different ways, from marketing and recruitment to finances and event planning.

I learned that leadership wasn't about doing everything myself. It was about communicating clearly, helping people understand what needed to happen, and giving them opportunities to contribute.

That lesson became even more meaningful during my Summer 2025 internship at Northwestern Mutual.

While working with the Illustrations team, I wrote my first Jira user story for a small text change in a platform. Before presenting it, I spent time researching how to write a good user story because I wanted to make sure the requirement was clear.

I then presented it to a group of roughly 15 engineers and other stakeholders. Seeing that small change move from a written requirement to an implemented update helped me appreciate how much communication goes into building software.

The idea itself wasn't particularly complicated. The important part was describing the change clearly enough that the people responsible for implementing it understood what needed to happen.

I saw similar lessons during my Summer 2026 internship at Veolia, where I worked with user stories and technical documentation as part of a larger SAP transformation effort.

Across these experiences, I began to appreciate how much work happens between identifying a need and delivering a solution.

Product management involves more than coming up with ideas. It requires translating needs into actionable requirements, working with different teams, and helping everyone move toward a shared goal.

ACM gave me an early opportunity to practice some of those skills in a student organization. My internships helped me see how they apply in a professional environment.

## 3. Success is about the experience you create, not just what you deliver

Growing ACM from around five active members to approximately 30 was exciting. We organized workshops, welcomed guest speakers, and created opportunities for students to learn outside the classroom.

But the part I'm proudest of isn't a particular event or membership milestone.

It's the community we built.

I wanted ACM to be a place where students could get involved, meet people who shared their interests, and find opportunities to grow. Whether someone wanted help with a résumé, wanted to practice solving coding problems, or was interested in exploring a new area of computer science, I wanted them to have a reason to participate.

When I asked former officers about their experiences, their responses reminded me that people got different things out of the organization.

Jordan Miller, who served as our marketing lead, pointed out that recruiting students was only the beginning. What made people want to stay was feeling that others were interested in them and wanted them to feel included. Giving members leadership opportunities also helped them become more invested in the club.

Karl Kiefer IV, who served as our secretary and now is the VP of the club, emphasized the relationships he built and the people who inspired him. For him, ACM offered something beyond technical knowledge: an opportunity to step outside his comfort zone, learn from other students, and grow in ways that extended beyond the classroom.

Ian Cooper brought another perspective. He valued opportunities like LeetCode nights, where members worked together to solve problems, and technical presentations that introduced students to subjects they might not encounter in class. He also believed ACM benefited from challenging its members and encouraging them to explore difficult topics, even when they didn't understand everything right away.

Those perspectives stuck with me because they showed that ACM's value couldn't be measured by attendance alone.

People came to learn, but they also found community, inspiration, and opportunities to challenge themselves. Not everyone needed the same experience, and that's part of what made the organization valuable.

I think about that when considering product management, too.

A product can launch with an impressive list of features and still fall short if it doesn't help people accomplish what they need to do. Its value isn't determined solely by what a team delivers. It also depends on how people experience it and what they gain from using it.

ACM wasn't a commercial product, of course. But the underlying question was similar: Were we creating something people genuinely valued?

For me, success meant more than getting students to show up. It meant creating an environment where they could build relationships, discover new interests, contribute their own skills, and keep growing.

## What this taught me about product management

Looking back, I didn't become interested in product management because of one particular workshop or leadership experience. My interest developed through a combination of experiences: helping rebuild ACM, working with technical teams during my internships, and seeing how ideas become real solutions.

For anyone unfamiliar with the field, product management is about understanding what users need, defining what a product should accomplish, and working with cross-functional teams to turn those needs into solutions.

It requires a combination of skills: listening, prioritization, communication, collaboration, and the ability to think about both the bigger picture and the details required to make progress.

Leading ACM gave me an opportunity to start developing some of those skills before I fully understood how closely they connected to the work I wanted to pursue.

It also taught me that building something meaningful isn't a solo effort. It takes people willing to share ideas, take responsibility, learn from one another, and work toward a common goal.

I'm grateful to the officers and members who helped make ACM what it became. I may have stepped into the presidency, but the organization wouldn't have grown without the people who gave their time, brought their ideas, and made it a community worth being part of.

And if you're a UW-Whitewater student interested in computing, career development, or simply meeting people who share your interests, I'd encourage you to check out ACM.

Sometimes, the experiences that teach you the most about your future career start with something as simple as deciding to join a club.

For me, that decision started with wanting to become a member. What followed helped shape the way I think about leadership, building things for people, and the kind of product manager I hope to become.
