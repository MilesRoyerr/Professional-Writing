# Documentation Is a Product Skill, Not Just a Paperwork Task

**Originally published:** November 3, 2026  
**Category:** Product Management  
**Publication status:** Published

When people think about internships, they sometimes imagine someone getting coffee, taking notes, or doing the work nobody else wants to do.

But what if some of that work is more important than people realize?

Think about the last time you attended a meeting for a student organization. Someone probably took notes, recorded decisions, and wrote down what needed to happen next.

Or think about going grocery shopping. You make a list because you don't want to forget something you need.

Even in basketball, some of the most valuable contributions don't come from scoring the most points.

Think about Draymond Green and the Golden State Warriors. People sometimes focus on his scoring numbers, but his defense, playmaking, rebounding, and ability to help his teammates succeed have been essential to the team's championship success.

These examples have something in common: **the work that helps everything else succeed isn't always the work that gets the most attention.**

I think the same thing applies to documentation in Product Management.

Documentation might not be the finished product, but it helps teams understand what they're building, why they're building it, and what needs to happen next.

And without that understanding, even a great product idea can struggle to become reality.

### My First User Story Was About Adding One Letter

My first experience writing a user story was during my internship at Northwestern Mutual.

The request was simple: add an "s" to some fine print in a document.

I wrote the user story in Jira and presented it to engineers and stakeholders. Afterward, I got to see someone take that story and implement the change.

It was a small request, but I learned something from the experience.

I found myself overthinking the story and trying to make it perfect. I wanted to make sure I had everything right.

But ultimately, the goal wasn't to write the most impressive user story. It was to communicate the requested change clearly enough that the team could understand it and act on it.

That experience helped me begin to understand what user stories are supposed to accomplish.

A user story describes a need from the user's perspective. It helps a team understand who needs something, what they need, and why it matters.

A common format is:

"As a [type of user], I want [something], so that [benefit]."

Of course, writing the sentence is only part of the work. You still need to understand the underlying problem, clarify the requirements, and determine what a successful outcome looks like.

But the story gives the team a starting point.

And that brings me to something I wanted to get better at.

### Why Business Analytics?

When I realized Product Management was the direction I wanted to take my career, I started thinking about the skills I needed to develop.

One that stood out was requirements gathering.

From conversations with other product managers and my own experiences, I was learning how important it is to understand what different stakeholders need and translate those needs into actionable requirements.

So when I found the SAP Business Analyst internship at Veolia, I saw an opportunity to develop that skill.

The job description aligned with what I wanted to learn, I connected with the company's purpose and mission, and I saw an opportunity to work alongside talented people.

During the internship, I supported an SAP Ariba Supplier Lifecycle & Performance implementation.

Some of my responsibilities included working on user stories, supplier registration workflows, process mapping, and requirements documentation.

At Northwestern Mutual, I had used Jira. At Veolia, I worked with Google Sheets.

The tools were different, but the underlying challenge was similar: understand what people needed, document the requirements, and help translate business processes into something the implementation team could work with.

I also gained a better appreciation for how different stakeholders can approach the same process from different perspectives.

What seems straightforward to one person might raise questions for another. Requirements can overlap. Details need clarification. Sometimes, you have to work through a problem before you can clearly articulate what the solution needs to accomplish.

**Documentation became more than writing things down. It became a way to help people develop a shared understanding of what needed to happen.**

### Documentation Helps You Think Through a Problem

Looking back at these experiences, I've come to appreciate that documentation does more than communicate information. It can help you identify what you still need to learn.

Imagine a team discussing a new product feature. Everyone seems to agree on what needs to happen, and the conversation moves quickly.

But when you sit down to document the requirements, you realize there are questions nobody has answered.

Who is this feature for? What problem are we trying to solve? What does the team need to accomplish before moving forward? Are there assumptions that need to be validated?

Writing those questions down makes the gaps in your understanding easier to recognize.

And that's a good thing.

You don't want to discover that everyone had a different interpretation of a requirement after the engineering team has already built the feature.

Documentation gives you an opportunity to clarify those details, ask better questions, and establish what the team needs to know before committing to a solution.

It also helps with prioritization.

Think of documentation as a working to-do list for a product team. When you're planning a sprint or preparing for a product launch, you need to understand what needs to get done, what the priorities are, and what questions still need answers.

Good documentation helps make those things visible.

But it's about more than checking items off a list. It's about understanding why each task matters, how it connects to the bigger picture, and what needs to happen before the team can move forward.

**Documentation isn't just a record of your thinking. It can help improve your thinking.**

### Understanding the User Comes Before Documenting the Solution

There's another distinction I've been learning about as I develop my understanding of Product Management.

A feature request and a user need aren't necessarily the same thing.

Imagine a customer asks for a button that lets them download a report.

You could document that request, communicate it to the engineering team, and have the feature implemented exactly as requested.

But what if you asked one more question?

Why does the customer need to download the report?

Maybe they need to share it with their manager. Perhaps they need to analyze the information somewhere else. Or maybe downloading the report is exactly what they need.

Understanding the reason behind the request gives you more information to evaluate potential solutions.

The customer's suggestion might be the right solution, but you shouldn't assume that without understanding the problem.

This is where documentation and communication work together.

As a product manager, you need to ask questions, gather information, and understand the needs you're trying to address. Then, you need to communicate that understanding so the team can determine what to build and why.

Documentation can capture what you've learned, clarify the requirements, and identify questions that still need answers.

It doesn't guarantee that you've identified the right problem or chosen the best solution. But it can help the team recognize assumptions, evaluate alternatives, and work from a clearer understanding of the user's needs.

### Sometimes, the Most Important Work Isn't the Most Exciting

Think back to Draymond Green and the Warriors.

His contributions go beyond scoring. His defense, playmaking, rebounding, and communication help create opportunities for the rest of the team.

Product Management documentation can play a similar role.

People tend to focus on the features being released, not necessarily the requirements, process maps, and decisions that helped make those features possible.

It's similar to software migration. Moving an existing system to a new platform might not be as exciting as launching a new feature, but it can still be necessary for a system to remain maintainable and support future improvements.

Documentation might not be the most glamorous part of building a product, either.

But that doesn't make it unimportant.

A team needs people who can understand requirements, document decisions, communicate changes, and help everyone stay aligned on what needs to happen next.

At the same time, documentation isn't valuable simply because someone created it. A document that nobody uses or understands isn't helping the team succeed.

The goal isn't to produce paperwork for its own sake. It's to do the work that helps the team build something useful.

### More Documentation Doesn't Always Mean Better Documentation

There's one more lesson I've taken away from these experiences.

Good documentation is about usefulness, not volume.

You can have a massive collection of user stories and still struggle to identify what actually matters.

Some requirements might overlap. Others might no longer be relevant. And a document that nobody references isn't doing much to help the team.

The goal isn't to write everything down just because you can.

It's to capture the information people need to understand the problem, make decisions, and do their work.

Sometimes that means a detailed process map. Sometimes it's a well-defined user story. Other times, it's a few clear notes documenting a decision made during a meeting.

The format matters less than whether the information serves its purpose.

And that's something I wish I had understood when I was writing my first user story at Northwestern Mutual.

I didn't need to make a small request more complicated than it was. I needed to understand it, communicate it clearly, and give the team what they needed to move forward.

### My Biggest Takeaway

I'm still growing in my understanding of Product Management, particularly when it comes to identifying user needs, gathering requirements, and understanding how different pieces of a product fit together.

But my experiences at Northwestern Mutual and Veolia have taught me to appreciate the role documentation plays in that process.

Documentation and communication go hand in hand. You can understand a problem, but if you can't communicate that understanding to the people responsible for solving it, getting everyone moving in the same direction becomes much harder.

Taking notes, asking questions, documenting decisions, and clarifying requirements might not be the most exciting parts of building a product. But they help teams understand what they're working toward and why it matters.

So, if you're aspiring to become a product manager, take the time to develop your documentation skills.

And if you're already a product manager mentoring someone who's interested in the field, encourage them to develop those skills, too.

Building a successful product requires more than identifying a need. You also have to help a team understand that need, determine how to address it, and communicate what needs to happen next.

**Sometimes, building a better product starts with asking a better question and taking the time to write down the answer.**
