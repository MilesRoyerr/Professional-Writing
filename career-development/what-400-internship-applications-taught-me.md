# What 400 Internship Applications Taught Me About Interviewing (That Nobody Really Talks About)
 
**Author:** Miles Royer  
**Originally published:** October 20, 2026  
**Category:** Career Development  
**Original publication:** [Read the article on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/what-400-internship-applications-taught-me-nobody-really-miles-royer-2ohac)

---

When I started college, I thought I had a pretty good idea of what I wanted to do with my career.

Four years, 400+ internship applications, countless interviews, and four internships later, I've learned that I had a lot to figure out.

I've interviewed for positions I thought I wanted, overlooked opportunities that turned out to be great, lost an offer because I took too long to make a decision, and discovered career interests I didn't even know I had.

Everyone talks about the STAR method, researching companies, and preparing for common interview questions. Those things matter, and I recommend learning them.

But there are other lessons I've learned through experience that I wish someone had told me when I was a freshman.

Here are some of them.

### 1. Don't overlook a company just because you haven't heard of it.

When I first started applying for internships, I made a mistake that seems obvious to me now: If I hadn't heard of a company, I wasn't particularly interested in interviewing with them.

I assumed I had a pretty good understanding of which companies were worth pursuing and which ones weren't.

I was wrong.

My first internship was with Generac, a company I hadn't even heard of before applying. I ended up having a great experience there, and it taught me an important lesson about how little I actually knew about the opportunities available to me.

I was judging companies based on name recognition rather than the work I could do, the people I could learn from, or the experience I could gain.

Don't let your lack of familiarity with a company become the reason you miss out on a great opportunity.

Research the organization. Look at the position, the responsibilities, the team, and what you could learn. A recognizable name doesn't automatically make an internship great, and an unfamiliar name doesn't automatically make it a bad one.

Give yourself the chance to find out.

### 2. When companies say applications are reviewed on a rolling basis, they mean it.

One of the biggest misconceptions I had about internships was that if I didn't land something early, I had missed my chance.

I've learned that recruiting timelines can look very different from one company to another.

Here's a look at when I secured my four internships:

- **Generac:** May 2023, just a couple of weeks before I started.
- **Johnson Controls:** I interviewed in person for about 30 minutes before Thanksgiving, received an offer a couple of weeks later, and accepted it on the spot.
- **Northwestern Mutual:** March 2025, after exploring software engineering positions and discovering an opportunity in digital product management.
- **Veolia:** Early April 2026, when I accepted an SAP Business Analyst internship.

Four internships. Four different recruiting experiences.

My first internship became available because someone who had originally been hired for the role left to pursue another opportunity. That opened the door for me, even though I was applying very late in the process.

And that is something I wish more students understood.

Many companies begin recruiting for summer internships months in advance, sometimes as early as July or August of the preceding year. Applying early is absolutely worthwhile.

But that doesn't mean every position is filled by March.

Candidates decline offers, circumstances change, and new opportunities can emerge. Some employers continue hiring well into the spring, and occasionally even into May.

Apply early, but don't stop applying just because you think you're late.

This is especially important for freshmen and sophomores who might not have the same recruiting opportunities as upperclassmen.

You never know when a position will open up or when a company will be willing to take a chance on someone with less experience.

### 3. You don't need to meet every qualification to be worth interviewing.

When you're a freshman or sophomore, looking at internship descriptions can be intimidating.

You might see a long list of programming languages, tools, certifications, and preferred qualifications and immediately assume you're not qualified.

Don't automatically count yourself out.

For many early-career internships, employers aren't just evaluating what you already know. They're also evaluating your potential.

Can you communicate effectively? Are you curious? Do you take initiative? Can you explain why you're interested in the position? Are you willing to learn?

Those qualities matter, particularly when a company is hiring someone who is still early in their education.

Of course, some positions have firm requirements, and you shouldn't misrepresent your skills. But a preferred qualification isn't always a dealbreaker.

When you're discussing your experience, I recommend balancing what you've already accomplished with what you're working toward.

Talk about the classes you've taken, the projects you've built, and the skills you've developed. Then explain what you want to learn, the certifications you're pursuing, or the direction you hope to take your career.

You don't need to pretend you've already mastered everything.

You need to demonstrate that you have a foundation to build on and a genuine interest in doing so.

Your current skills show what you can contribute today. Your initiative helps demonstrate what you could contribute tomorrow.

Just make sure you follow through. If you tell an interviewer you're going to develop a skill, take steps to develop it.

### 4. Don't accept an internship just because someone offers it to you.

This is one of the harder lessons I've learned, because it came from a mistake I still think about.

I once interviewed in person for an IT support position at a law firm. The interview went well, and the team seemed to like me.

But I was unsure whether the position was right for me.

At that point, I thought I knew what I wanted to do, but I was still figuring out my interests and career direction. I took time to think about the opportunity and talk with people in career services and others I trusted.

Eventually, I decided I wanted the position and was ready to accept it.

There was just one problem.

About a week had passed, and the recruiter contacted me to let me know that the offer had been rescinded. They had given the position to another candidate who was ready to move forward.

I had taken too long to decide.

I don't blame the company. They had a position to fill, and they found someone who was ready to accept it.

But that experience changed how I approach interviews and offers.

If you're genuinely interested in a position, take the process seriously. Research the role, ask questions, and work through your hesitations before you reach the final interview.

If you discover that the opportunity isn't right for you, it's okay to withdraw from the process professionally. You don't need to continue interviewing simply because you were invited.

If you do proceed to the final round, you should have a reasonably clear understanding of whether you'd seriously consider accepting an offer.

And if an offer arrives, don't assume it will remain available indefinitely.

My personal rule of thumb is to aim to make a decision within three days when possible, while respecting the employer's stated deadline. If you need more time, communicate that promptly and ask whether an extension is possible.

An offer is an opportunity, not a guarantee that the employer will wait forever.

I still wonder what would have happened if I'd accepted that law firm position. Would I have discovered my interests sooner, or would I have gone down a different path entirely?

I'll never know.

What I do know is that the experience taught me to be more intentional about the opportunities I pursue and more decisive when the right one comes along.

### 5. Have a reason for wanting the role beyond wanting an internship.

Getting an internship is exciting. But getting an internship that actually aligns with your interests and goals is even better.

Before applying or interviewing, take time to think about what you're looking for.

What interests you about the work? What skills do you want to develop? What kind of team do you want to work with? How does this opportunity fit into your broader career goals?

I've found it helpful to use AI tools like ChatGPT to compare job descriptions with my resume, interests, and career goals. These tools can help identify relevant qualifications, potential mismatches, and questions worth investigating.

But don't let AI make the decision for you. Verify what it tells you, research the company independently, and talk to people who understand the role.

I've encountered internships that initially looked appealing but seemed much less aligned with my goals after I looked more closely at the responsibilities.

For the past two summers, I've been more intentional about finding positions that aligned with what I wanted to learn and the direction I wanted to pursue.

That made a difference in how much I enjoyed the experiences.

Don't just ask whether you can get the internship. Ask whether it's an internship you actually want.

### 6. Start networking as soon as you accept the offer.

One of the best things I did after accepting my internship at Veolia was start connecting with people at the company on LinkedIn.

In the months before my internship began, I reached out to employees, learned about their roles, and started conversations with people I might otherwise not have met until I arrived.

When I finally walked into the office, I was meeting plenty of people for the first time. But there were also people who already knew who I was.

We had talked before. They knew what I was interested in, what I was excited about, and even some of the things I was nervous about.

It made those first interactions feel more natural and helped me start building relationships before my first day.

You don't have to wait until you start working to learn about the organization.

Look for alumni from your school, employees in similar positions, and people whose career paths interest you. Ask thoughtful questions, learn about their experiences, and build genuine connections.

You don't need to ask every person for a referral or a job lead. Sometimes, the most valuable thing you can do is learn from someone who has already been where you're trying to go.

And networking shouldn't stop when you accept an offer.

Keep learning about the organization, the team, and the work you'll be doing. You'll be better prepared to contribute when you arrive.

### 7. The best interview preparation starts with understanding your own story.

Everyone talks about the STAR method, and for good reason. Learning how to structure your answers can help you communicate your experiences more effectively.

But I think there's an important step that comes before memorizing frameworks or practicing common interview questions.

You need to understand your own story.

Before one of my successful internship interviews, I spent time reviewing my resume, thinking about my experiences, and identifying what I could bring to the company.

I thought about growing up in Brazil, why I had switched majors, what I had learned from previous internships, and which experiences connected most closely to the position.

Then I looked at the job description and considered how my background aligned with what the company was looking for.

When the hiring manager asked me, "Tell me about yourself," I was ready.

I could explain my background, connect my experiences to the position, and communicate what interested me about the opportunity.

The conversation went well, and I received an offer the next day.

That experience reinforced something I now consider essential to interview preparation.

You don't need to memorize an answer to every question an interviewer might ask. You need to understand your experiences well enough to explain them in different contexts.

Review your resume and LinkedIn profile. Think about what you enjoyed in previous roles, what you learned, what challenged you, and what you want to do next.

Then connect those experiences to the position you're pursuing.

The STAR method can help you organize your answers. But no framework can replace knowing what you bring to the table.

### 8. Keep applying until you've accepted an offer.

Finally, don't stop applying just because you're waiting to hear back from a company.

An interview that went well doesn't guarantee an offer. A recruiter saying they'll be in touch doesn't mean you're the selected candidate. Even reaching a final round doesn't guarantee you'll get the job.

Keep exploring opportunities while you're waiting.

That doesn't mean you need to apply indiscriminately to every posting you see. Be strategic about the roles you pursue and make sure they align with your interests and qualifications.

But don't put your search on hold because you're optimistic about one opportunity.

The internship market can be competitive, and waiting around for a single employer to make a decision can cost you other opportunities.

If you've already accepted an offer, honor your commitments and understand the implications before making any changes. Until then, keep moving forward.

You can't control every hiring decision, but you can control how you prepare, how you communicate, and whether you continue looking for opportunities.

### Final thoughts

Looking back at my first internship search, I realize how much I didn't understand about recruiting.

I overlooked companies because I didn't recognize their names. I underestimated how late opportunities could become available. I didn't always know what I wanted, and at one point, I lost an offer because I took too long to make a decision.

But each experience taught me something that helped me approach the next opportunity differently.

Four internships later, I still have plenty to learn. I don't have a perfect formula for getting hired, and I don't think one exists.

What I do know is that you don't need to have everything figured out before you start interviewing.

Be willing to explore unfamiliar opportunities. Demonstrate your potential. Prepare by understanding your own story. Ask questions. Build relationships. And when an opportunity aligns with what you're looking for, be ready to make a decision.

Your first internship doesn't have to define your entire career. Sometimes, it just needs to help you discover what you want to do next.
