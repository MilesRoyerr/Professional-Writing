# One Year After Switching Majors: What I've Learned So Far
  
**Author:** Miles Royer  
**Originally published:** August 3, 2026  
**Category:** Career Development  
**Original publication:** [Read the article on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/one-year-after-switching-majors-what-ive-learned-so-far-miles-royer-hohdc)

---

One year ago, I made one of the biggest decisions of my college career.

After spending three years studying Computer Science, I transferred to the University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee and switched my major to Information Science & Technology.

At the time, it felt like I was changing directions.

Looking back, I realize I was really finding my direction.

This past year has taught me more than I expected, not just about technology, but about myself.

### Why I Started in Computer Science

I didn't choose Computer Science because I dreamed of becoming a software engineer.

I chose it because I loved technology.

Growing up, I spent countless hours playing games like Minecraft and Clash of Clans. Those experiences made me curious about the people behind the products I loved.

I didn't know anyone personally who worked in tech, so I started reaching out to professionals on LinkedIn. Sometimes I'd even look through the credits of my favorite games, search for developers online, and ask them about their careers.

To my surprise, many of them replied.

Those conversations shaped my understanding of the industry long before I ever stepped into a college classroom.

Like many students around 2019 and 2020, Computer Science seemed like the obvious path into tech, so I pursued it wholeheartedly.

I attended career fairs, applied for hundreds of internships, collected plenty of rejection emails, and eventually landed opportunities that helped me begin my career.

Every rejection taught me something.

Every conversation expanded my perspective.

And every internship helped me understand the industry a little better.

### Discovering What Energized Me

As I continued through school, I noticed something.

I enjoyed programming.

I respected software engineering.

But when I looked at what energized me outside the classroom, it wasn't always writing code.

I loved building communities.

At the University of Wisconsin-Whitewater, I had the opportunity to help grow our ACM student chapter from a small group into an active community of students who enjoyed learning together.

I loved organizing events, connecting people, and helping others succeed.

At the time, I didn't recognize that those experiences were teaching me just as much about my future career as any programming assignment.

### The Internship That Changed My Perspective

One opportunity completely changed how I viewed my future.

I interviewed for a software engineering internship at Northwestern Mutual. While I wasn't selected for that role, I was invited to interview for a Digital Product internship instead.

I accepted, thinking it would simply make me a better software engineer.

Instead, it introduced me to product management.

For the first time, I saw a role that combined technology, communication, business, and collaboration.

I enjoyed writing user stories.

I enjoyed understanding customer needs.

I enjoyed working with engineers, business analysts, and product managers to solve problems together.

Most importantly, I realized that what excited me most wasn't just building software.

It was helping teams build the right software.

That internship didn't just change my résumé.

It changed how I thought about my career.

### A Difficult Interview That Gave Me Clarity

Not long afterward, I interviewed for another software engineering internship at a company I deeply respected.

I made it to the technical interview.

Then I froze.

The interviewer tried to guide me through the problem, but I couldn't put the pieces together.

Walking back to my car afterward, I kept replaying the interview in my head.

For a while, I wondered whether I simply wasn't good enough.

Eventually, I realized I had been asking myself the wrong question.

The real question wasn't:

> "Can I become a software engineer?"

It was:

> "Is software engineering where I create the most value?"

For me, the answer was no.

Admitting that wasn't failure.

It was clarity.

### One Year Later

A year after changing majors, I can confidently say it was the right decision for me.

Over the past year, I've had the opportunity to study communication, business systems, analytics, and product management while continuing to build on the technical foundation that Computer Science gave me.

This summer, I had the opportunity to join Veolia as an SAP Business Analyst Intern.

That experience has reinforced everything I discovered during my Digital Product internship.

I genuinely enjoy bringing people together, understanding business problems, and helping teams deliver solutions.

Every project has strengthened my confidence that product management is where I want to continue growing.

### What This Year Taught Me

If I could share a few lessons with students who are still figuring out their path, they would be these:

- Don't confuse loving technology with wanting the same career as everyone else.
- Don't let rejection convince you to stop trying. Every application teaches you something.
- Be curious. Reach out to people, ask questions, and learn from professionals whose work you admire.
- Internships teach you things that classrooms simply can't.
- Your first plan doesn't have to be your final plan.
- Changing directions isn't always a setback. Sometimes it's the first step toward finding where you belong.

Technology needs incredible software engineers.

It also needs product managers, project managers, business analysts, UX designers, cybersecurity professionals, data specialists, architects, and countless other roles.

The goal isn't to chase the title that sounds the most impressive.

It's to discover where your strengths create the greatest value.

### Looking Ahead

One thing hasn't changed since I started college.

I'm still fascinated by technology.

What's changed is how I hope to contribute to it.

I don't regret the three years I spent studying Computer Science.

Those years gave me technical foundations, introduced me to incredible mentors, and opened doors to internships that shaped my career.

Without that journey, I wouldn't be where I am today.

As I begin my final year at UW–Milwaukee, I'm excited to keep learning, keep asking questions, and keep growing as a future product manager.

This is also my first LinkedIn article, but it won't be my last.

I plan to continue sharing what I'm learning about product management, internships, leadership, and building a career in technology. If anything in this article resonated with you, I'd love to hear your perspective. Feel free to connect with me or share your own story, I'd enjoy continuing the conversation.

Here's to the next year of learning.
