# How I Turned a “Garbage” Resume Into One That Gets Noticed

**Author:** Miles Royer  
**Originally published:** September 24, 2026  
**Category:** Career Development  
**Original publication:** [Read the article on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/how-i-turned-garbage-resume-one-gets-noticed-miles-royer-ga6jc)

---

“Miles, your resume is garbage.”

Those were the words my mentor at my first internship at Generac told me when I showed him the resume I used to get the internship.

And honestly?

He was right.

At the time, my resume was basically a Word document with a few jobs, some descriptions of what I had done, and not much else. Somehow, it had gotten me my first internship.

But getting one internship with a resume doesn't mean your resume is good.

I looked at mine from my mentor's perspective, and I realized there was a lot of room for improvement.

So I did what any college student would do:

I asked people who were a few years ahead of me for advice.

One of my friends introduced me to Overleaf, I found a resume template I liked, and I completely overhauled my resume.

That became the foundation for the resume I've continued to build and modify throughout college.

Since then, I've had people tell me that my resume is organized, easy to read, and looks like I know what I'm doing.

The funny part?

I absolutely did not know what I was doing when I started.

I've just spent years learning, getting feedback, experimenting, and changing it.

So after reviewing hundreds of college students' resumes and constantly tinkering with my own, here are some of the biggest lessons I've learned.

### 1. Your resume is your first project

I think this is the most important mindset shift you can make.

Your resume isn't just a document.

It's your first project with the company.

Before you ever walk into an interview, the company has to decide whether your application is worth moving forward.

Your resume is one of the first pieces of work you're giving them.

It should communicate:

- Who you are
- What you've done
- What you've learned
- What you can contribute
- Why your experience is relevant to the role

I also think of a resume as a story.

It's the story of your education, experiences, projects, skills, and the things that have brought you to where you are today.

And most importantly, it should help tell the story of where you're going next.

### 2. Relevance beats volume

You don't need to put every single thing you've ever done on your resume.

If you're applying for your first internship, that doesn't mean you need to list every summer job, random position, or high school activity you've ever participated in.

Ask yourself:

“Does this experience help demonstrate why I would be a good fit for this opportunity?”

If yes, keep it.

If not, consider whether the space could be better used somewhere else.

Your resume has limited real estate.

Use it intentionally.

### 3. Show results, not just responsibilities

This is one of the biggest changes I've made to my own resume over the years.

A lot of people write bullets that simply explain what their job was.

For example:

**Before:**

> Provided front-line technical support for students and staff across campus housing, resolving Wi-Fi, network, and Duo issues, managing calls and closing tickets in ServiceNow.

That's not a bad bullet.

But we can make it much stronger.

**After:**

> Delivered front-line technical support to 1,000+ campus residents, resolving Wi-Fi, network, and Duo security issues to restore connectivity and minimize downtime.

> Managed and resolved IT tickets in ServiceNow, documenting issues and maintaining accurate records to meet resolution timelines.

> Translated technical issues into clear, user-friendly guidance, improving user satisfaction and reducing repeat support requests.

What's different?

The second version gives the reader more context about the scope, impact, and value of the work.

Instead of only asking:

“What did I do?”

Ask yourself:

“What changed because I did it?”

Whenever possible, think about:

**Action → Scope → Problem → Outcome**

And quantify your work when you can.

How many people did you help?

How many projects did you work on?

How much time did you save?

How many tickets did you resolve?

How large was the team?

How much money was involved?

Numbers give context.

### 4. Make your resume easy to read

Your resume doesn't need to win a graphic design award.

In fact, I'd argue that overly complicated resumes can sometimes work against you.

You want someone to be able to quickly find:

- Your name
- Your education
- Your experience
- Your projects
- Your skills
- Your accomplishments

Use consistent formatting.

Keep spacing clean.

Make headings obvious.

Don't cram every possible detail onto the page.

Good design isn't about making your resume flashy.

It's about making information easy to find.

### 5. Tailor your resume to the role

I don't believe you should have one resume that you send to every company.

Instead, I recommend having a strong master resume that contains your experiences and accomplishments.

Then tailor it.

Applying for a Product Management role?

Emphasize product work, stakeholder communication, requirements, project ownership, and outcomes.

Applying for a technical role?

Emphasize your technical projects, programming, systems, and technical accomplishments.

Applying for a business analyst role?

Emphasize process improvement, requirements gathering, data, documentation, and business systems.

You don't need to reinvent your entire resume every time.

You're simply changing which parts of your story you emphasize.

### 6. Use AI — but use it correctly

Yes, I think ChatGPT and other AI tools can be extremely useful for resumes.

But there's a big difference between:

> “Make this sound impressive.”

and

> “Here is what I actually did. Help me identify the scope, outcomes, and skills demonstrated by this experience, then turn that into concise resume bullets without inventing accomplishments.”

Use AI to help you articulate your experience.

Don't use it to manufacture experience you don't have.

Your resume should still be yours.

### 7. Keep a public and private version

This is something I learned the hard way.

If you're posting your resume publicly, you don't necessarily need to include your phone number.

I used to have a version of my resume online with my phone number attached.

Eventually, I started getting random calls from people I didn't know.

Not ideal.

Now, I recommend having a public version that removes information you don't want strangers to have.

Your private application version can contain the information necessary for employers.

Your public networking version can point people toward your LinkedIn, portfolio, or preferred contact method.

Think about what information you actually want sitting on the internet.

### 8. A couple of my controversial resume takes

Okay, this one might get me some disagreement.

#### You probably don't need a summary at the top.

If I'm meeting a recruiter at a career fair, I've probably already given them my elevator pitch.

They know my major.

They know what I'm looking for.

They know the type of role I'm interested in.

And then they open my resume and read the exact same information again.

Personally, I don't use a summary section.

I'd rather use that space to show what I've actually done.

That doesn't mean summaries are always bad.

There are situations where they can be useful, particularly for someone with a long or specialized career.

And if you really want to explain your story, that's where a cover letter or conversation can provide additional context.

But for many college students, I don't think a summary is necessary.

#### Your resume doesn't need to contain everything.

More information does not automatically make your resume better.

Sometimes the best edit you can make is deleting something.

### 9. Your resume is never finished

This might actually be the biggest lesson I've learned.

I've reviewed hundreds of college students' resumes, and one of the most common things I hear is:

> “Oh yeah, I edited this a couple months ago. It's up to date.”

Keep editing it.

Keep looking at it.

Keep finding ways to improve it.

Ask other students to look at it.

Ask professors.

Ask mentors.

Ask employers.

Ask people you've connected with on LinkedIn.

There are so many people willing to give resume feedback if you ask.

And you're going to get conflicting advice.

One person might tell you to remove something.

Another might tell you to add it.

Someone might tell you to move a section.

Someone else might tell you they would format it differently.

That's okay.

You don't have to follow every piece of advice you receive.

Take the feedback, figure out what makes sense for your story and your goals, and keep iterating.

I've changed my resume more times than I can count.

Every internship has changed it.

Every new project has changed it.

Every new job has changed it.

Every time I've learned something new about recruiting, I've looked at my resume differently.

Your resume should evolve as you do.
